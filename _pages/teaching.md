<h2>Teaching philosophy</h2>

<p>As a first-generation student and daughter of migrant Central American parents, I consider my academic struggles and achievements to be communal, shared experiences. It is through collaboration with family, peers of color, and social communities that I have survived, thrived, and pursued venues to return such support. My teaching experience with undergraduate and graduate students, coupled with my work experience in college access and success programs, demonstrates my engagement in multiple environments: from underfunded public high schools to an all-women's institution, to a Research 1 university, to technology companies and reading programs. I understand how crucial it is to make accessible the opportunities and knowledges that have been systematically hidden from underrepresented and under-resourced students of color. Through my personal and professional background, I have shaped my teaching philosophy. My pedagogical practices prioritize accessibility, transparency, and sustainability.</p>

<p>When considering accessibility, I aim to provide equitable resources so that students may make informed decisions about their academic progress. In my former teaching experience at the University of Illinois’ <a href="https://researchops.web.illinois.edu/opportunity/summer-research-opportunities-program-srop" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Summer Research Opportunities Program</a> (SROP), I led the Social Sciences cohort with students from liberal arts schools and research institutions (for many students, it was their first time thinking and learning about research and graduate school culture). Since I had also been an SROP fellow in 2014, I detailed my own personal experiences of imposter syndrome, microaggressions, isolation, and academic hazing. Additionally, I individually met with students weekly to check in about their research project and discuss class feedback. Aside from my syllabus lectures, I also held discussions and workshops to navigate library databases, financial aid, and professional development skills. Even while being in the same university setting, not all students arrive with identical knowledges to complete higher education. Unfamiliarity to hidden curriculums should not impede a student’s success, and my teaching style has molded into creating opportunities for strategies to support all my students.</p>

<p>Upon reinforcing access, I also advocate for transparency: in my teachings, I communicate bureaucratic intricacies about the hidden curriculum of academia. I firmly believe that gatekeeping information (such as the heteronormative, racist, sexist, and classist systems of academia) only reinforce the very violences that the academy attempts to condemn. I draw from James Baldwin’s (1963) critical analysis on education. Baldwin emphasizes that education should encourage a person “To ask questions of the universe, and then learn to live with those questions, [that] is the way he achieves his own identity,” however society prefers obedient citizens (18). Baldwin declares that the “obligation of anyone who thinks of himself as responsible is to examine society and try to change it and to fight it—at no matter what risk” (18). When I first meet students, I relay my ideology that lying or omitting information does not help them, and it certainly does not help me. I vocalize my teaching approaches and clarify my reasonings so that my students are fully aware of my academic intentions throughout the course.</p>

<p>As an educator, I have capitalized on my expertise of teaching by also working in industry and non-profit organizations. Such experiences have urged me to foster a commitment to mentorship, especially in developing mentorship support at the level students feel sustainable and comfortable. Therefore, my mentorship exists on intersections of compassion and discipline: I understand students’ external obligations (perhaps taking care of siblings and/or working extra shifts to help with tuition), while also keeping them accountable to deadlines that they feel challenging, but manageable. I view my students as scholars and thinkers; they are theorists of the world and phenomenon around them. I am excited to support the Mission College student community by equipping them with accessible, transparent, and sustainable tools so that they may continue to explore and build their potential, within and outside of the classroom.</p>
